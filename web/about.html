What is the Stroop effect?

In 1935 the psychologist John Ridley Stroop showed that people are slower and make more mistakes when they name the ink color of a word that spells a different color. Reading is so automatic for adults that the word pops into mind first, and the brain has to actively hold it back to report the color instead.

Why it matters for your mind

Selective attentionFocusing on the one feature that matters (the ink) while ignoring a strong distraction (the word).
Inhibitory controlStopping an automatic response before it happens: a core executive function behind self-control and decision making.
Processing speedHow quickly you take in information and respond. The timer and speed score make it visible.
Cognitive flexibilitySwitching smoothly between competing rules as colors and words change on every cell.
Sustained focusLarger boards ask you to stay steady for longer without a single slip.
Tracking progressPersonal bests and a reaction-time chart show how your speed and consistency change over time.

Your eyes and visual attention

  • Visual scanning: moving your gaze smoothly from word to word, row by row.
  • Color discrimination: telling similar hues apart quickly and accurately.
  • Eye and voice coordination: linking what you see to what you say, many times a minute.
  • Visual search load: bigger boards, up to 16 by 16, increase how much your eyes process at once.

Screen time is still screen time. Follow the 20-20-20 habit: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 metres) away for 20 seconds, and blink often.

Trusted by researchers and clinicians

Versions of the Stroop task are used worldwide in psychology and neuropsychology to study attention and executive function, including research on healthy ageing, attention difficulties, fatigue, stress and recovery.
